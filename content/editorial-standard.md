# The Jailyard Editorial Standard

What the written product is, and the bar it clears.

## The product

The Jailyard publishes a long-form column for each edition of the season: a
preseason edition, then one per week, in chronological order. **Power rankings
are the recurring editorial spine** of every edition: twelve teams, ordered by
judgment, each position argued from evidence a league member would recognize
as true and pointed.

Beyond the rankings, **each edition develops its own form from the stories and
evidence available that week.** An edition earns its sections; none are
required. If a week produces a great mailbag, write one. If it produces a
two-line obituary and a single perfect chat exchange, that can be the whole
frame. Structure follows material.

## The subject

Twelve people making decisions under uncertainty, building versions of
themselves out of football players before reality has a chance to respond.

The roster, the record, the trade that got made: that is the fact. What a man
believed when he did it, and what it says about him that he believed it, that
is the human being. The writing holds both and never lets go of either. All
fact and it is a box score. All interpretation and it is a feeling with no
body. The work is making the fact feel lived and the meaning feel true, with
no visible seam where the reporting stops and the reading starts.

The method a man uses to get out of his bind is the man. Everyone here is in
one. Naming the bind accurately and moving on is reporting; showing what he
does about it is the column.

**This is not a template.** Twelve compulsory psychological profiles is the old
uniformity in new clothes. Some capsules center a stated wager. Others want
close football mechanics, a trade relationship, comic interaction, franchise
history, a cultural frame, or a plain verdict. Different material, different
form, different length.

## The bar

Elite long-form sports-column quality:

- **Judgment.** The rankings take positions a reader could dispute, and every
  position is argued, not recited. A falsifiable call beats a safe summary.
- **Specificity.** Names, numbers, moments. Never three sentences in a row
  without a concrete detail.
- **Rhythm.** Sentence length varies. Sections breathe where the story is big
  and move fast where it is thin. Where the writer lingers is itself an
  opinion.
- **Range.** An obligation of the edition, not of every section. The writing
  goes down into a thing until it comes apart into its moving parts, trusting
  the reader to follow, and it pulls all the way out to what a thing weighs:
  why it matters, what it costs, what it reveals. An edition written at one
  altitude for its whole length has failed this even if every paragraph is
  good.
- **Surprise.** At least one thing per edition the reader did not see coming:
  an angle, a juxtaposition, a call. Humor is the columnist's job, not only
  the league's. An owner may still land the best line in the piece; the
  standard is that the column repeatedly _creates_ comedy through framing,
  juxtaposition, escalation and payoff rather than only reproducing funny
  quotes.
- **Owner-level attention.** Every owner is seen. The reader finds their team
  treated with real attention whether they won by forty or benched their
  quarterback.
- **Fair, sharp comedy.** Roasts land on rosters and decisions, never on
  people's lives. The owner laughs; the owner never winces.

No named-writer imitation. No mechanical pattern quotas. The voice is the
league's own, grown from its real chat and its real history.

## Sources

Every fact, quote, and storyline comes from a real, citable source dated at or
before the edition's cutoff. The registry of writer inputs is
`content/writer-inputs.json`; anything untraceable to a registered source
class does not ship. Chat quotes are verbatim. Numbers come from the data.
New coinages are free; once published they are callable forever.

### What needs a source and what does not

The rule binds **facts, quotes, and storylines**. It does not bind the writing
itself. Original metaphor, parody, coinage, humor, and ordinary allusion are
authored expression and need no registered source. A checkable outside factual
predicate carried inside one still does: if the joke asserts that a thing
happened, that thing needs admissible support dated at or before the cutoff,
and anything the reader could look up and find false is a defect no matter how
well it lands.

### Interpretive warrant

Every gate checks whether a stated fact is admissible. None checks whether an
attributed belief is earned. A page of invented inner lives passes every gate
green, and that is a worse failure than dullness, because dullness is visible
and fabrication is not. So the reach of a reading is bounded by its evidence:

| Evidence in hand                     | What may be written                                 |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Direct words plus a decision he made | An attributed belief. What he thought, why he moved |
| Repeated observable conduct          | A qualified characterization. A pattern, a tendency |
| A roster or transaction alone        | "bets as if", "behaves as if". Never private motive |
| Thin                                 | An open question. Never a fabricated arc            |

Writing the open question is a legitimate move and often the better one. What
is never legitimate is filling the gap with an invented interior.

## Style constraints

- No em dashes.
- No "it's not X, it's Y" constructions or other phrasing that reads
  machine-made.
- The prose never discusses how the column gets made.
- No emoji in prose (verbatim chat quotes keep whatever they contain).
- The audience is sharp, college-educated league members from LA and San
  Diego who know ball. Write up to them.

## The reader test

A league member reads the edition, laughs at least once, screenshots
something, forwards it, and disputes at least one ranking out loud. That is a
pass. Anything less is a draft.
