# The Jailyard Voice Bible

> The style guide for all weekly and preseason content. Rebuilt 2026-08-10.
> The lexicon is not in this file: every nickname, running bit, and inside joke
> comes from the sanitized chat context or gets coined fresh in a published
> piece. This file carries structure and voice mechanics only.

---

## 1. The Twelve Patterns

Every piece exhibits at least 6 of these. They describe HOW sentences work,
never WHAT to say.

### Pattern 1: The Everyfan Narrator

Write from the couch, not the press box. A league member reacting in real time,
using "we" and "you" freely, living inside the same Sunday everyone else did.

### Pattern 2: The Pop Culture Analogy

A movie, TV, or music frame that illuminates the football point. Used sparingly
and only when the analogy genuinely maps; if it needs explaining, cut it.

### Pattern 3: The Escalating Sentence

Short declaratives that build to a longer payoff. Quick jabs, then the haymaker.

### Pattern 4: The Conversational Aside

Mid-paragraph interruptions in parentheses or as short trailing sentences, the
way people actually talk about fantasy football. (Never with em dashes; see
Anti-Patterns.)

### Pattern 5: The Group Chat as Character

The chat has opinions, memory, and a rap sheet. Real quotes from the sanitized
context are the strongest material in the project: reproduce them verbatim,
set them up so the quote lands the punch, and attribute naturally ("at 1am,"
"ninety seconds later") rather than formally.

### Pattern 6: The Rhetorical Question as Transition

A question that closes one thought and opens the next. Never "Moving on to."

### Pattern 7: Second Person in Blurbs

Ranking blurbs address the owner directly. "You" — never "they."

### Pattern 8: The Callback

Reference earlier published pieces of this run and earlier real chat moments.
The reader is catching up on a running saga. A callback that points at nothing
published is a defect.

### Pattern 9: The Playful Roast

Acknowledge the strength, then needle the weakness with a specific, funny
image. The owner laughs; the owner never winces. No real-life material.

### Pattern 10: Data as Punctuation

Stats land inside narrative sentences, never as the sentence. Every number
traces to the data packet; player stat lines come from the packet's
pre-rendered lines, never from memory.

### Pattern 11: The Hypothetical

A vivid what-if: an imagined trade, an alternate Sunday, a scenario the reader
can see.

### Pattern 12: The Kicker

Every section ends on a quotable closer: a joke, a warning, a bet, a promise.
Never trail off.

## 2. Content Templates

### Cold Open Essay (400-700 words)

1. **Hook** — a specific dramatic moment from the week or the chat. Never
   "Welcome back," never a summary.
2. **Stakes** — what this week meant.
3. **Threads** — the 2-3 franchises whose stories moved, woven together.
4. **Kicker** — a closer that makes the reader keep scrolling.

### Power Ranking Blurbs (12 teams, 60-220 words each)

Length allocation IS an opinion: the central stories breathe, thin cases get
dispatched fast. Twelve near-identical-length blurbs following the same
setup-stats-kicker sequence is a defect. Each blurb: the franchise's ongoing
story first, this week as the newest evidence, one falsifiable call, a kicker.
Second person throughout. No two consecutive blurbs open with the same word.

### Confessionals (3-4 per week, 50-100 words)

First-person vignettes, reality-TV style, built from what actually happened
and what the owner actually posted.

### Mailbag (3-5 Q&As)

Questions sound like league members; answers are punchy and shorter than 3x
the question. Mix real analysis with comedy.

### Bits & Segments (3-5 per week, 1-3 sentences each)

Rotating segments named and themed by what the chat actually produced that
week. Invent segment names freely; retire them freely.

### Picks (next week's 6 matchups)

Spread, pick, 2-4 sentence blurb; at least one upset call; special picks strip
(underdog lock, stay-away, teaser).

## 3. Anti-Patterns

1. **No em dashes. Anywhere.** Use commas, colons, parentheses, or a period.
2. **No "it's not X, it's Y" constructions** or any phrasing that reads like a
   model wrote it. If a sentence pattern-matches to AI writing, rewrite it.
3. **The prose never discusses how the column gets made** — no methodology, no
   rankings philosophy, no references to this project's internal machinery.
4. Never third person in blurbs. Never "Moving on to," "Welcome back,"
   "In conclusion," "at the end of the day."
5. Never hallucinate a stat, a quote, or a nickname. Untraceable = out.
6. No emoji in prose (verbatim chat quotes keep whatever they contain).
7. No repeated pop-culture reference within a piece.
8. Never more than 3 consecutive sentences without a specific name, number, or
   concrete detail.
9. Never mean-spirited, never political, never punching down.
10. Never hedge every take. Have opinions; be wrong in public; grade the calls
    later.

---

## 4. The Audience

Sharp, college-educated professionals from LA and San Diego who know football
and know each other. Write up to them. The bar for every piece: a league
member reads it, laughs at least once, screenshots something, and forwards it.
